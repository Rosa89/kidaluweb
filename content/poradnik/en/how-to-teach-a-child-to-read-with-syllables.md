Many children learn to read through syllables, and for good reason. A syllable already sounds like a piece of a real word, while single letters stay abstract for a five-year-old. A child who masters a few dozen syllables can read thousands of words. Below we describe how to walk this road at home, without pressure and without any teaching background.

## Why syllables and not letters

When a child learns single letters and tries to blend them, they hit a trap: "c", "a", "t" said separately sound like "kuh-a-tuh", not "cat". The child has to strip the extra sounds away in their head, and that is hard at five. The syllable "ca" already sounds like part of a real word. Add "t" and the word is done.

The syllable method avoids this problem. The child first learns the vowels, then open syllables, meaning a consonant plus a vowel: "ma", "ta", "la". From these they build the first words: "mama", "papa", "lala". Success comes fast, and that success drives the rest of the learning.

## Where to start

**Step 1. Vowels.** Begin with a, e, i, o, u. Show the letter and say it long and sing-song. Hunt for vowels in family names and on packaging in the kitchen. This stage can take a week or a month, it does not matter.

**Step 2. Open syllables with one consonant.** Pick a consonant whose sound is easy to stretch, such as "m" or "l". Practise "ma, me, mi, mo, mu". Only when the child reads them fluently, add the next consonant.

**Step 3. First two-syllable words.** "Mama", "lala", "papa", "tomato" in parts. The child reads syllable by syllable, then repeats the whole thing faster. The moment two sounds turn into a word with meaning is a real discovery for children.

**Step 4. Closed syllables and longer words.** Only now introduce syllables that end in a consonant: "cat", "dog", "sun". Then three-syllable words and short sentences.

## How much to practise

Five to ten minutes a day gives more than an hour once a week. A five-year-old can focus on one task for a few minutes and then needs a change. It is better to stop while they still want more than to push on until they start fidgeting. Then the next day they will ask for more themselves.

A fixed time works well, for example after dinner or before the bedtime story. A ritual saves the parent from negotiating and saves the child from feeling that learning is a punishment.

## What to avoid

- **Correcting every mistake.** When the child reads "mota" instead of "mama", ask: "Is that a word?" Let them spot the slip themselves.
- **Comparing with siblings or friends.** Every child has their own pace. A six-year-old who is starting to read is not behind a five-year-old who already reads.
- **Skipping stages.** If open syllables are not yet fluent, words with closed syllables will be frustrating.
- **Material rewards.** A sticker for every word stops working quickly. The real reward is the label on their favourite juice, read all by themselves.

## When an app makes sense

An app does not replace a parent, but it complements practice well. It takes over what is hard at home: dozens of repetitions of the same syllable without boredom, instant feedback, and a gentle rise in difficulty. The parent keeps what matters most: reading together, talking about the text, and the joy when the child reads their first sentence.

When choosing an app, check that it leads the child along the same road you do: from vowels, through open syllables, to words. If it throws the child straight into whole sentences or mixes methods, it will get in the way rather than help.
